\section{EMC in Power Electronics}

Electromagnetic compatibility (EMC) describes the ability of electrical and electronic equipment to operate as intended without causing or experiencing unacceptable electromagnetic interference. 
An EMC problem generally involves three elements: a noise source, a propagation path, and a noise sink. 
The noise source generates electromagnetic disturbances, the propagation path transfers them through conducted or radiated coupling, and the sink responds to the resulting disturbance. 
This basic principle is illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:BlockDiagram_SourceVictim}, where the propagation path is separated through a measurement port.
This is used as either a measurement of the emitted disturbance from the source or as input port for a immunity test of the noise sink.
\begin{figure}
	\centering\import{./figures/2_Fundamentals/}{BlockDiagram_SourceVictim.pdf_tex}
	\caption{Block diagram of EM interaction between source, propagation path, and sink.}
	\label{fig:BlockDiagram_SourceVictim}
\end{figure}

In a power-electronic converter, the switching semiconductor devices are the dominant noise sources, while the converter layout, passive components, cables, and enclosure determine the available propagation paths. 
Other circuits or sensors connected to, or located near, the converter may act as victims.
This situation is particularly relevant in electric vehicles, where high-power inverters are located close to sensitive electronic components and sensors, such as those used for safety applications and autonomous-driving systems.
In this context, EM interference must be effectively mitigated to ensure the reliable and safe operation of the vehicle.

The comprehensive fundamentals of EMC, including coupling mechanisms, measurement methods, and compliance requirements, are covered in the classical EMC literature and should be consulted for a detailed treatment. This section instead provides the background needed for the remainder of this thesis by focusing on the EM emission behavior of the noise source -- the power-electronic converter. 
In particular, it explains how switching semiconductor devices generate the voltage and current transients that form the basis of the converter's electromagnetic emissions. 
The subsequent discussion then considers how these emissions propagate through the converter and appear at measurement ports.

\subsection{Emission Generation}
The rapid voltage and current changes produced by switching power-semiconductor devices excite electromagnetic disturbances in the surrounding circuit.
Understanding their frequency content requires relating the repetition rate and shape of the switching waveform to its spectrum.
Following the graphical approach presented by Costa~\cite{graphical_costa_2008}, this subsection first establishes the spectrum of a periodic rectangular pulse and then examines the effects of finite transition times, waveform asymmetry, and resonance.
Gaussian smoothing provides a theoretical reference for the influence of transition smoothness, while a simulated waveform illustrates the combined effects and the need for experimental validation.
The analysis concerns the switching-voltage spectrum at the source, rather than the emissions at a system port, which additionally depend on the propagation paths discussed in the next subsection.

\subsubsection*{From a Switching Pulse to a Periodic Spectrum}
\textbf{Rectangular Pulse:} The rectangular pulse provides the simplest representation of a switching waveform and serves as the starting point for the analysis:

\begin{equation}
	s(t) = \mi{V}{DC}\cdot\text{rect}\left(\frac{t}{\text{d}\mi{T}{s}}\right), 
\end{equation}
where \mi{V}{DC} is the DC-link voltage, $\text{d}$ is the duty cycle, and \mi{T}{s} is the switching period.
Using the Fourier-transform convention with kernel $e^{-j2\pi ft}$ gives
\begin{equation}
	S(f) = \mi{V}{DC}\cdot\text{d}\mi{T}{s}\cdot\text{si}\left(\pi f\text{d}\mi{T}{s}\right),
\end{equation}
where $\text{si}(x) = \sin(x)/x$ and $\text{si}(0) = 1$.
This expression describes the continuous spectrum of an isolated pulse and has units of voltage multiplied by time; it must therefore be distinguished from the voltage amplitudes of a periodic signal.

\textbf{Periodic Repetition:} To represent steady-state switching at a fixed duty cycle and switching frequency, the isolated pulse is repeated by convolution with a Dirac comb:
\begin{equation}
	\mi{s}{PC}(t) = s(t) * \sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} \delta(t-n\mi{T}{s}).
\end{equation}
With $\mi{f}{s} = 1/\mi{T}{s}$, its Fourier transform becomes
\begin{equation}
	\mi{S}{PC}(f) = \sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{S(n\mi{f}{s})}{\mi{T}{s}}\,\delta(f-n\mi{f}{s}).
\end{equation}
Periodic repetition thus produces discrete spectral lines at integer multiples of the switching frequency.
Their complex Fourier-series coefficients are $S(n\mi{f}{s})/\mi{T}{s}$, with the DC coefficient equal to the mean voltage $\text{d}\mi{V}{DC}$.
The magnitudes of these coefficients sample the continuous function $A(f) = |S(f)|/\mi{T}{s}$.
The following discussion uses this two-sided convention; in a one-sided peak-amplitude spectrum, the magnitudes at positive frequencies are doubled, while the DC component remains unchanged.

\textbf{Spectral Envelope:} For the graphical analysis, $A(f)$ is expressed relative to a voltage reference \mi{V}{ref} and plotted against logarithmic frequency:
\begin{equation}
	A_\text{dB}(f) = 20\log_{10}\left(\frac{A(f)}{\mi{V}{ref}}\right).
\end{equation}
Choosing $\mi{V}{ref} = \SI{1}{\micro\volt}$ gives a level in dB\textmu V.
The low-frequency asymptote is $\text{d}\mi{V}{DC}$, while the high-frequency envelope decreases in proportion to $1/f$, corresponding to \SI{-20}{dB/dec}.
Their intersection defines the first corner frequency,
\begin{equation}
	\mi{f}{cut1} = \frac{1}{\pi\text{d}\mi{T}{s}}.
\end{equation}
The corner marks the intersection of two asymptotes, rather than a sharp change in the exact spectrum, which contains the lobes and zeros of the si function.
This distinction between the spectral envelope and individual spectral components underlies the graphical representations, beginning with the rectangular waveform in \fref{fig:Diss_SpectrumCreation_zeroOrder}.

\subsubsection*{Influence of the Transition Shape}
\textbf{Equal Rise and Fall Times:} The rectangular approximation neglects the finite switching speed of real semiconductor devices.
Introducing linear transitions of equal duration \mi{t}{r} gives a trapezoidal pulse, obtained by convolving the rectangular pulse with a unit-area rectangular smoothing kernel:
\begin{equation}
	s(t) = \mi{V}{DC}\cdot\text{rect}\left(\frac{t}{\text{d}\mi{T}{s}}\right) *\frac{1}{\mi{t}{r}} \text{rect}\left(\frac{t}{\mi{t}{r}}\right).
\end{equation}
The convolution smooths the switching edges while preserving the pulse area.
Convolution in the time domain corresponds to multiplication in the frequency domain:
\begin{equation}
	S(f) = \mi{V}{DC}\cdot\text{d}\mi{T}{s}\cdot\text{si}\left(\pi f\text{d}\mi{T}{s}\right) \cdot \text{si}\left(\pi f \mi{t}{r}\right)
\end{equation}
The second si factor introduces an additional corner frequency,
\begin{equation}
	\mi{f}{cut2} = \frac{1}{\pi\mi{t}{r}}.
\end{equation}
For $\mi{t}{r} \ll \text{d}\mi{T}{s}$, the envelope has three distinct regions: an approximately constant low-frequency region, a \SI{-20}{dB/dec} region, and a \SI{-40}{dB/dec} region above \mi{f}{cut2}.
As illustrated in \fref{fig:Diss_SpectrumCreation_firstOrder}, the pulse duration and transition duration therefore control different parts of the envelope: the former sets the first corner, while the latter determines the onset of the additional high-frequency attenuation.

\textbf{Unequal Rise and Fall Times:}
Power-semiconductor devices generally exhibit different switching speeds during turn-on and turn-off, resulting in unequal rise and fall times.
To account for this asymmetry, the convolution-based approximation introduces separate transition-shaping terms for the rising and falling edges:
\begin{equation}
	s(t) = \mi{V}{DC} \cdot \text{rect}\left(\frac{t}{\text{d}\mi{T}{s}}\right) * \left(\mi{s}{sw,r} + \mi{s}{sw,f}\right)
\end{equation}
where \mi{s}{sw,r} and \mi{s}{sw,f} represent the respective transition-shaping terms.
In the graphical spectral approximation, the corresponding contributions are expressed as the sum of two si~functions:
\begin{equation}
	S(f) = \mi{V}{DC}\cdot\text{d}\mi{T}{s}\cdot\text{si}\left(\pi f\text{d}\mi{T}{s}\right) \cdot \left(\text{si}\left(\pi f \mi{t}{r}\right) + \text{si}\left(\pi f \mi{t}{f}\right)\right)
\end{equation}

The two transition durations introduce distinct characteristic frequencies, $1/(\pi\mi{t}{r})$ and $1/(\pi\mi{t}{f})$.
The longer transition begins to attenuate its spectral contribution at a lower frequency, allowing the faster edge to dominate between the two corners.
Above both corners, the asymptotic envelope decreases at \SI{-40}{dB/dec}.
Thus, unequal rise and fall times modify the intermediate-frequency behavior without changing the high-frequency asymptotic slope, as illustrated in \fref{fig:Diss_SpectrumCreation_asymTr}.

\textbf{Two-Slope Transitions:}
Whereas the preceding approximation distinguishes the rising and falling edges, a two-slope approximation accounts for changes in slope within an individual transition.
During turn-on, for example, the initial drain-source voltage change is influenced by the commutation-loop inductance and the current rise, while the subsequent voltage fall is governed by the gate drive and device capacitances.
This behavior is represented by convolving the rectangular pulse with the sum of two rectangular smoothing functions of different durations:
\begin{equation}
	s(t) = \mi{V}{DC} \cdot \text{rect}\left(\frac{t}{\text{d}\mi{T}{s}}\right) * \left(\frac{1}{\mi{t}{r1}} \text{rect}\left(\frac{t}{\mi{t}{r1}}\right) + \frac{1}{\mi{t}{r2}} \text{rect}\left(\frac{t}{\mi{t}{r2}}\right)\right)
\end{equation}
The corresponding frequency-domain expression is
\begin{equation}
	S(f) = \mi{V}{DC}\cdot\text{d}\mi{T}{s}\cdot\text{si}\left(\pi f\text{d}\mi{T}{s}\right) \cdot \left(\text{si}\left(\pi f \mi{t}{r1}\right) + \text{si}\left(\pi f \mi{t}{r2}\right)\right)
\end{equation}

This expression has the same mathematical form as the preceding graphical approximation, but the two durations now characterize contributions within a switching edge rather than separate rising and falling edges.
Their influence on the spectral envelope is illustrated in \fref{fig:Diss_SpectrumCreation_twoTr}.

\subsubsection*{Resonant Contributions}
Whereas the preceding models describe monotonic transitions, practical switching waveforms often exhibit overshoot followed by damped oscillations.
The underlying resonances arise from the interaction between the commutation-loop inductance and the output capacitance of the device in the off state.
Their excitation adds spectral contributions at the resonance frequencies, with the magnitude and width governed by the excitation strength and damping.
Differences in the effective capacitances and circuit conditions during turn-on and turn-off can produce distinct oscillation frequencies \mi{f}{on} and \mi{f}{off}, as modeled and predicted in~\cite{xu} and observed in the measurements presented in this thesis.
\fref{fig:Diss_SpectrumCreation_res} illustrates the resulting modification of the switching-edge spectrum.
\begin{figure}[h]

Taken together, the transition and resonance models explain individual features of the spectrum without reproducing the complete switching process.
Voltage-dependent capacitances and interactions between the gate and power circuits further modify the waveform, limiting the quantitative accuracy of these idealized representations.

\subsubsection*{Gaussian Smoothing as a Theoretical Reference}
The preceding models also show why transition duration alone is insufficient to characterize high-frequency emissions: spectral decay depends on waveform smoothness.
Piecewise-linear waveforms have discontinuities in their first derivatives, resulting in high-frequency spectral envelopes that decrease in proportion to $1/f^2$.
For comparison, convolution with a Gaussian kernel produces an infinitely smooth waveform:
\begin{equation}
	s(t) = \mi{V}{DC} \cdot \text{rect}\left(\frac{t}{\text{d}\mi{T}{s}}\right) * \left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma_t}e^{-\frac{t^2}{2\sigma_t^2}}\right),
\end{equation}
where $\sigma_t$ is the standard deviation of the smoothing kernel.
Its Fourier transform yields
\begin{equation}
	S(f) = \mi{V}{DC} \cdot \text{d}\mi{T}{s} \cdot \text{si}(\pi f\text{d}\mi{T}{s}) \cdot e^{-2\pi^2\sigma_t^2 f^2}.
\end{equation}
The Gaussian factor decays faster than any inverse power of frequency, contributing a term proportional to $-f^2$ to the logarithmic magnitude rather than a constant slope in dB/dec.
In graphical terms, its slope becomes increasingly steep as frequency increases, rather than approaching the constant slope of the piecewise-linear approximations.
\fref{fig:Diss_SpectrumCreation_Heisenberg} illustrates this distinction from the piecewise-linear models.

As discussed by Patin~\cite{Patin_HeisenbergGabor}, the Gaussian kernel attains the Heisenberg-Gabor minimum time-frequency uncertainty product when temporal and spectral widths are defined by their RMS spreads.
In this sense, it achieves the narrowest spectral spread for a given temporal spread.
To distinguish the benefit of waveform shaping from that of simply slowing the transition, comparisons must use a consistent measure of transition duration.
Because the Gaussian function has no finite beginning or end, an effective duration, such as the 10\%--90\% transition interval, is needed for practical comparisons with finite ramps.

While this represents the mathematical optimum for emitted EM noise, this switching transient would be catastrophic for switching losses.
This shape prolongs the interval during which substantial voltage and current are simultaneously present in the device.
The Gaussian waveform therefore provides a theoretical reference for spectral concentration, while practical optimization must balance the spectral benefit against switching losses.

The regions labeled ``DC'', ``Operating Point'', and ``Transient'' in \fref{fig:IntroFullSpec} highlight the dominant influences on the spectrum and the corresponding possibilities for modifying it.

In the ``Operating Point'' region, the spectrum can be modified through the switching frequency, duty cycle, and modulation strategy.
In the ``Transient'' region, the duration and shape of the switching edges and the associated oscillations become particularly important, making the gate drive, device characteristics, and circuit parasitics relevant parameters for spectral optimization.
These regions indicate dominant influences rather than an exclusive separation: changes in operating conditions can also affect the switching transients, while the DC-link voltage scales the spectrum as a whole.

\textbf{Limits of the Prediction:}
A physically plausible time-domain waveform does not establish the accuracy of its high-frequency spectrum, particularly above the dominant switching resonance.
The first limitation concerns model validation: calibration against conventional double-pulse measurements does not validate spectral components below the measurement noise floor, as discussed in the upcoming Subsection~\ref{sec:MeasurementLimitation}.
Agreement in switching time and overshoot can therefore coexist with substantial errors in much smaller high-frequency components.
The second limitation concerns numerical processing: solver tolerances, time steps, resampling, and the FFT record length and window all affect the computed spectrum.
Piecewise-linear interpolation, for example, can introduce derivative discontinuities and an artificial high-frequency tail.
The peak near \SI{900}{MHz} in \fref{fig:IntroFullSpec} illustrates the need to distinguish physical effects from numerical artifacts.
No physical mechanism could be identified to explain this peak, and it could be altered by changing the solver settings, indicating a numerical rather than a physical origin.
Such features should therefore be checked by refining the simulation settings, for example by reducing the time step and tightening solver tolerances.
Even if the spectrum remains unchanged under these checks, comparison with measurements is still required to validate the underlying model.

The graphical models provide a basis for interpreting the source spectrum, and simulation combines the underlying mechanisms within a circuit model.
Establishing the accuracy of the resulting predictions nevertheless requires measurements with sufficient bandwidth and dynamic range to resolve the relevant spectral components.
In the absence of a suitable measurement environment, none of the models considered here has yet been experimentally validated for predicting these small high-frequency spectral components.
Providing this experimental basis for model validation and the subsequent optimization of power-semiconductor switching behavior is a central aim of this thesis.

\subsection{Propagation Paths in a Generic Inverter Application}
The switching transients discussed above excite noise currents that propagate through the inverter and its connected components to the external system ports.
The resulting conducted emissions depend on both the semiconductor switching behavior and the frequency-dependent impedances of these propagation paths.
Although filtering and careful layout can reduce the coupling to external systems, complete suppression over the entire frequency range is not practically achievable.
Characterizing the noise source therefore remains essential for understanding and mitigating system-level emissions.

\fref{fig:ESB_CMDM_Propagation} shows a simplified equivalent circuit for one phase of a three-phase machine inverter.
The circuit comprises an inverter half-bridge, the DC supply cables, and a machine represented by its winding impedance, the back electromotive force induced by rotor rotation, and parasitic capacitances to ground.
In an electric vehicle, this ground reference typically corresponds to the chassis rather than either DC supply conductor.
A line impedance stabilization network (LISN) is inserted between the power supply and the device under test (DUT) to establish a defined, frequency-dependent impedance within the specified measurement band and to attenuate disturbances from the supply.
In the illustrated equivalent circuit, the LISN is represented by a $\pi$-type filter with a series inductor and shunt capacitive branches.
The series $RC$ branch on the DUT side couples high-frequency disturbances to the measurement port, where the voltage across the termination resistance is measured.
This voltage is related to the noise current through the LISN transfer impedance; it is not a direct measurement of the total chassis current.

\begin{figure}[h]
	\centering
	\import{./figures/2_Fundamentals/}{Diss_ESB_CMDM_Propagation.pdf_tex}
	\caption{Equivalent circuit of the common-mode and differential-mode noise propagation paths.}
	\label{fig:ESB_CMDM_Propagation}
\end{figure}

The conducted noise currents at the DC supply interface can be decomposed into differential-mode (DM) and common-mode (CM) components.
DM currents flow in opposite directions in the two supply conductors and close their loop through the power circuit, without requiring a ground return path.
They arise from switching-related fluctuations in the inverter input current; their propagation toward the supply depends, among other factors, on the impedance of the DC-link capacitor and the supply wiring.
CM currents, by contrast, flow in the same direction in both supply conductors and return through the chassis and capacitive or intentional connections to ground.

The parasitic capacitances \mi{C}{DC+} and \mi{C}{DC-} couple the positive and negative DC rails to ground, while \mi{C}{SwN} and \mi{C}{SP} represent the ground coupling of the switching node and the machine star point, respectively.
Rapid changes in the voltage across these capacitances drive displacement currents according to $i_C = C\,\sddt{\mi{v}{C}}$.
Their contributions therefore depend on both the capacitance values and the local voltage transients, rather than on capacitance alone.
The relative capacitance values are application-dependent, so no universal ordering can be assumed.
For equal DC-rail capacitances, equal and opposite rail-voltage variations relative to ground produce cancelling ground-current contributions.
If both rail voltages instead rise or fall together relative to the chassis, the currents through the two capacitances flow in the same direction and add rather than cancel, thereby contributing to the common-mode current even when the capacitances are equal.

The switching-node capacitance provides a direct coupling path from the rapidly changing switching voltage to the chassis.
In contrast, noise currents flowing through the machine star-point capacitance must first pass through the motor windings.
The winding impedance can therefore limit the current through this path, with the degree of attenuation depending on frequency.
Consequently, a larger star-point capacitance does not necessarily imply a larger contribution at the measurement port.
The chassis currents divide among the available return paths according to their impedances; in the illustrated setup, the LISN branches and DC supply cables provide defined paths back to the inverter.
This lumped equivalent circuit illustrates the principal coupling mechanisms, but accurate high-frequency predictions for a real system require a more detailed model that accounts for distributed cable and winding effects.

For the modal decomposition, \mi{I}{LISNp} and \mi{I}{LISNn} denote the noise currents in the positive and negative DC supply conductors at the LISN interface, with both reference directions chosen from the supply toward the inverter.
The DM and CM components are then defined as
\begin{equation}
	\mi{I}{DM} = \frac{\mi{I}{LISNp} - \mi{I}{LISNn}}{2}
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
	\mi{I}{CM} = \frac{\mi{I}{LISNp} + \mi{I}{LISNn}}{2}.
\end{equation}
With this convention, \mi{I}{CM} is the common-mode component per conductor, and the sum of the two conductor currents is $2\mi{I}{CM}$.
Applying these equations to measured currents separates the DM and CM contributions at the LISN interface.
Because this separation relies on adding and subtracting the two currents, their relative timing or phase must be known.
These relations apply to instantaneous currents or complex frequency-domain quantities, not to independently measured spectral magnitudes, because the relative phase must be retained.
The resulting DM and CM spectra each reflect both the excitation by the semiconductor devices and the transfer through the corresponding propagation paths.

\subsection{Standardized EMC Measurements and Limits}
Standardized emission measurements provide reproducible conditions for assessing the disturbances generated by a device under test (DUT).
CISPR, the International Special Committee on Radio Interference within the International Electrotechnical Commission (IEC), develops standards for such measurements and their limits.
For automotive applications, CISPR~25 specifies methods and limits for conducted and radiated disturbances.
The CISPR~16 series provides basic specifications for measuring apparatus and measurement methods, which are referenced where applicable by product standards.

Conducted emission measurements assess disturbances coupled into the connected wiring.
In the voltage method, a LISN establishes a defined supply impedance and couples the disturbance voltage to a measurement receiver.
Radiated emission measurements instead use antennas to measure the electromagnetic field produced by the DUT and its wiring under specified test conditions.
The antenna type, position, and polarization, together with the arrangement of the DUT and cables, are prescribed to obtain reproducible results.

In many EMC test schemes, conducted emissions are assessed up to \SI{30}{\mega\hertz}, while radiated measurements cover higher frequencies into the gigahertz range.
This division is not universal: the applicable frequency ranges depend on the product standard and measurement method, and automotive conducted-emission tests can extend beyond \SI{30}{\mega\hertz}.

Automotive inverter and converter suppliers may also be required to meet customer-specific conducted-emission limits up to \SI{100}{\mega\hertz} or higher.
Such requirements can supplement standardized tests with additional frequency ranges or stricter limits defined by the vehicle manufacturer rather than by the standard itself.
Their purpose is to reduce interference risks during component integration and to provide margin for the final vehicle-level EMC assessment.
Compliance at component level therefore supports, but does not guarantee, compliance of the complete vehicle, whose emission behavior also depends on the installed wiring and interactions between components.

The measured levels are compared with frequency-dependent limits for the applicable test method and limit class.
These limits must be used together with the specified receiver bandwidth and detector, such as peak, quasi-peak, or average, as applicable.
A simulated FFT spectrum cannot therefore be compared directly with a compliance limit unless the measurement conditions and receiver response are represented consistently.
