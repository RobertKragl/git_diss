\section{Oversampling and Averaging}
The previous section discussed how small high-frequency components fall below the noise floor quickly after the resonance frequency.
As a first countermeasure, classical digital noise-reduction techniques are applied: oversampling and averaging.

Oversampling and subsequent averaging increases the effective number of bits (ENOB)~\cite{silicon_laboratories_an118_2013}:

\begin{equation}
	\mti{N}{ENOB} = \mti{N}{ADC} + 0.5\cdot \log_2\left(\frac{\mi{f}{s}}{2\mi{f}{max}}\right)
\end{equation}

Here, \mti{N}{ADC} is the nominal ADC resolution, \mi{f}{s} is the sampling frequency, and \mi{f}{max} is the highest frequency of interest.
The factor of 2 follows from the Nyquist criterion.
In practice, this improvement is limited by the maximum sampling rate of the oscilloscope and by the required analysis bandwidth, which is set to \SI{500}{MHz} in this work.

In addition, the effective noise floor can be reduced by recording a pulse chain (PC) with $M$ pulses instead of a single pulse (SP).
For FFT-based evaluation, this increases the number of samples and lowers the thermal-noise floor by

\begin{equation}
	\mi{V}{th,PC} = \frac{\mi{V}{th,SP}}{\sqrt{M}}
\end{equation}

according to Eq.~\ref{eq:RMStoAmplitudePlot}.
Consequently, the SNR improves by the same factor $\sqrt{M}$, because random noise is spread over the spectrum while deterministic switching components remain at fixed frequencies (multiples of \mi{f}{sw}).
The maximum usable value of $M$ is constrained by the oscilloscope single-shot memory.

Figure~\ref{fig:QuantNoise} illustrates this behavior by comparing a single-pulse spectrum $Q(\mi{V}{DS,SP}+\mi{V}{n,th})$ with a 100-pulse spectrum $Q(\mi{V}{DS,PC}+\mi{V}{n,th})$ and with the corresponding non-quantized pulse chain $\mi{V}{DS,PC}+\mi{V}{n,th}$.
An SNR improvement of about \SI{20}{dB} is obtained for the pulse chain.
However, once thermal noise is sufficiently suppressed, quantization noise becomes dominant and limits further improvement.
Hence, as also reported in~\cite{silicon_laboratories_an118_2013}, oversampling and averaging are effective only while thermal noise dominates and remains larger than the quantization step.
To access the full EMC-relevant frequency range, an additional measure is required.
