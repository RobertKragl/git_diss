\section{Error Sources in Dynamic Measurements of Power Electronics}
\label{sec:fundamental_measurement_errors}
To investigate the intrinsic electromagnetic behavior of a power semiconductor device, its switching behavior must be characterized under representative operating conditions. 
This requires measuring voltage and current at the device terminals as it switches between high-voltage blocking and high-current conduction.

The measurement system must resolve fast transitions while tolerating large signal amplitudes and common-mode disturbances.
Probe loading can change the switching process itself, whereas finite bandwidth, nonlinear response, and parasitic coupling can distort its recorded representation.
Distinguishing these mechanisms is essential because a plausible time-domain waveform does not establish the accuracy of its small high-frequency spectral components.

\fref{fig:ESB_MeasurementError} illustrates these mechanisms for a high-side voltage measurement and a low-side current-shunt measurement in a half-bridge.
The quantities of interest are the voltages and currents at the device power terminals in the absence of measurement-induced perturbations.
They are not directly available as error-free references; verification must instead combine probe characterization with independent measurements and an assessment of their uncertainties.
The following discussion establishes the design and verification criteria for the dual-channel differential active voltage probe and active current shunt developed in this thesis.
\tref{tab:dynamic_measurements} summarizes these criteria.

\subsubsection*{Probe Loading and Circuit Interaction}
Any measurement sensor presents a finite impedance to the circuit under test and can therefore alter the quantity being measured. 
A voltage probe is connected in parallel with the circuit and should have a high input impedance, whereas a current sensor is inserted in series and should add as little impedance as possible.

For drain-source voltage measurements, the differential input capacitance of the probe is connected in parallel with the device output capacitance.
The latter can be on the order of hundreds of picofarads, depending on the device and bias voltage, so a small probe capacitance may have only a limited relative influence.
Nevertheless, the strongly voltage-dependent output capacitance must be considered throughout the transition.
Capacitance from the probe terminals to ground creates an additional common-mode path and must be assessed separately, particularly when the unperturbed switching-node capacitance to ground is small.
% Streit_ECCE_Asia
                  
Current measurement in the commutation loop presents a different challenge. 
The loop inductance is deliberately minimized because it contributes to switching overvoltage and losses, and can be only a few nanohenries. 
The parasitic inductance introduced by a series current shunt may therefore be comparable to the loop inductance itself and perturb the current transient being measured.

The significance of a sensor's ohmic resistance also depends on the measurement objective.
Its effect on the main switching transient may be secondary to that of parasitic inductance, yet the added resistance can change the damping of oscillations and therefore their spectral amplitudes.
This distinction is particularly important for EMC-relevant measurements and is discussed further in Section~\ref{ch:AShunt}.

	\label{fig:Fundamental_Loading_TDFD}

As a first screening criterion, the magnitude of a voltage probe's input impedance should be at least ten times the source impedance magnitude over the frequency range of interest.
Conversely, the series impedance magnitude of a current sensor should be at most one tenth of the relevant circuit impedance magnitude.
These ratios are rules of thumb, not accuracy guarantees: reactive loading can shift resonances, and the relevant impedances can change during switching.
The permissible loading must therefore be established for the actual circuit and measurement objective.

Figure~\ref{fig:Fundamental_Loading_TDFD} compares the simulated response with and without a probe capacitance equal to 10\% of $\mi{C}{oss}(\SI{800}{V})$. 
The switching-node voltage changes as charge is transferred between the high- and low-side MOSFET output capacitances; for a given commutation current, the larger capacitance limits the voltage slope. 
Here, the turned-on MOSFET's $\mi{C}{oss}(\SI{0}{V})$ is much larger than both the turned-off MOSFET's $\mi{C}{oss}(\SI{800}{V})$ and the probe capacitance, so the simulated transient is barely affected. 
In the frequency domain, however, the probe capacitance shifts the resonance between the off-state MOSFET capacitance and the commutation-loop inductance.

\subsubsection*{Small-Signal Response and Bandwidth Criteria}
The small-signal response describes how a measurement probe transfers input signals over frequency when operated within its linear range. 
Its transfer function describes frequency-dependent gain and phase shift between input and output.
As discussed in Section~\ref{sec:Fundamental_Generation}, switching transients have broad spectra without a sharp upper frequency limit.
The measurement objective therefore defines a finite frequency band within which the probe response must be characterized.

If the probe bandwidth is insufficient, higher-frequency components are attenuated, which can make measured edges appear slower and can suppress high-frequency ringing. 
Gain variation within the frequency range of interest can likewise alter the relative amplitudes of spectral components. 
Frequency-dependent phase shift can further change the reconstructed waveform. Thus, a probe may produce a plausible waveform while still misrepresenting the amplitude or timing of fast features.



Required bandwidth follows the maximum frequency of interest, not the instrument's maximum displayed frequency.
A common criterion is that the probe's $-3$\,dB bandwidth exceed this maximum frequency. 
This criterion places the frequency of interest below the point at which the response magnitude has fallen to approximately $0.707$ of its reference value; it does not, by itself, guarantee flat gain or negligible phase distortion throughout the band. 

Small-signal frequency response is typically characterized with a vector network analyzer (VNA), which measures the probe's transfer response under a low-level sinusoidal excitation over a swept frequency range. 
This test characterizes linear frequency-dependent behavior; it does not establish that the probe remains linear under large input amplitudes, which must be assessed separately.

Figure~\ref{fig:Fundamental_Bandwidth_TDFD} illustrates the effect of limiting the probe bandwidth to \SI{100}{MHz} when measuring a fast switching transient.
The limited bandwidth slightly damps the main resonance and introduces a small delay in the measured transient, but otherwise has little effect on its shape.
In the frequency domain, it attenuates spectral components above \SI{100}{MHz}.
Because these components have low amplitudes, their attenuation has little effect on the overall time-domain waveform.

\subsubsection*{Large-Signal Response and Nonlinearities}

Small-signal characterization alone does not establish that a probe remains accurate at operating signal levels. 
VNA measurements generally use excitation well below the amplitudes encountered at power-device terminals.
Active sensors may become nonlinear as their input or output stages approach voltage or current limits. 
Finite slew rate and overload recovery can also distort fast transitions. 
Magnetic sensors may exhibit hysteresis or saturation, making their response dependent on signal amplitude and, in the case of hysteresis, on the sensor's prior magnetic state. 
These effects can cause gain compression, clipping, altered edge shapes, or delayed settling, none of which can be inferred from a low-level frequency-response measurement.

Large-signal verification should therefore use excitation that reproduces the signal amplitudes and transient speeds expected in operation, and compare the probe output with an independent reference measurement. 
Such a test can reveal gain compression, clipping, hysteresis, and overload recovery. 
Its conclusions are limited to the tested maximum amplitude, \mi{\hat{V}}{meas,max}, and minimum rise time, \mi{t}{r,meas,min}; subsequent measurements should remain below the amplitude limit and above the minimum rise time. 
Generating signals at the required amplitudes and rise times, and obtaining a reliable reference measurement, can be challenging.


\subsubsection*{Parasitic Coupling Paths}
Parasitic coupling provides unintended paths for switching transients to enter a sensor without passing through its intended sensing element. 
In a half-bridge, fast voltage transitions drive displacement currents through stray capacitances, while rapidly changing commutation-loop currents induce voltages through mutual inductance. 
Figure~\ref{fig:ESB_MeasurementError} illustrates two types of parasitic coupling: capacitive coupling, represented by \mi{C}{\sigma}, and inductive coupling, represented by the mutual inductances \mi{L}{\mathrm{m,i}} and \mi{L}{\mathrm{m,v}}. 
These are examples of possible coupling mechanisms; the figure is not exhaustive.

For the current-sensing arrangement in \fref{fig:ESB_MeasurementError}, the sense voltage comprises the desired shunt voltage and the parasitic contributions:
\begin{equation}
    \mi{v}{sense} = \mi{R}{shunt} \mi{i}{S} - \mi{L}{\mathrm{m,i}} \ddt{\mi{i}{S}} - \mi{R}{shunt}\mi{C}{\sigma}  \ddt{\mi{v}{DS}}
    \label{eq:parasitic_coupling_current_sense}
\end{equation}
assuming an ohmic shunt and the lumped coupling paths and reference directions shown in the figure.
The second term represents inductive pickup from the commutation current. 
The final term represents capacitive feedthrough from the switching node to the shunt's negative terminal.

	\caption{Simulation results of inductive coupling with \SI{1}{nH} and a coupling factor of 1.}

Figure~\ref{fig:Fundamental_Coupling_TDFD} shows the simulated effect of inductive coupling with a mutual inductance of \SI{1}{nH} and a coupling factor of 1. 
In the time domain, the coupled signal exhibits additional overshoot and oscillation relative to the true signal. 
Despite these differences, its overall shape remains plausible for the circuit's expected behavior, making the coupling error difficult to identify in the laboratory.

Comparison with an independent reference or controlled changes to the coupling geometry can help identify this error.
A discrepancy alone does not establish which measurement is inaccurate, while agreement cannot exclude errors shared by both arrangements.
Reference measurements must therefore be assessed for their own loading and coupling sensitivity.

\subsubsection*{Transmission-Line Effects}
Measurement cables possess distributed resistance, inductance, capacitance, and dielectric conductance. 
Once the cable length is no longer negligible compared with the wavelength of the signal components of interest, it must be treated as a transmission line rather than as an ideal connection. 
The characteristic impedance is
\begin{equation}
    \mi{Z}{W}(\omega) = \sqrt{\frac{R' + j\omega L'}{G' + j\omega C'}},
\end{equation}
where $R'$, $L'$, $C'$, and $G'$ denote resistance, inductance, capacitance, and conductance per unit length, respectively.
For a low-loss line, with $R' \ll \omega L'$ and $G' \ll \omega C'$, the propagation velocity and wavelength are approximately
\begin{equation}
    \mi{c}{W} \approx \frac{1}{\sqrt{L'C'}},
    \qquad
    \lambda \approx \frac{\mi{c}{W}}{f}.
\end{equation}
A cable of length $l$ thus introduces a one-way propagation delay $\tau \approx l/\mi{c}{W}$.

At each cable termination, any mismatch between the termination impedance and \mi{Z}{W} causes part of the incident voltage wave to be reflected. 
The voltage reflection coefficient at termination $k$ is
\begin{equation}
    \mi{\Gamma}{k}(\omega) = \frac{\mi{Z}{k}(\omega) - \mi{Z}{W}(\omega)}{\mi{Z}{k}(\omega) + \mi{Z}{W}(\omega)},
    \qquad k \in \{L,Q\},
\end{equation}
where \mi{Z}{L} is the load impedance and \mi{Z}{Q} is the effective MOSFET impedance seen by the cable, as shown in \fref{fig:TransmissionLineMeasurementCable}. 
In a drain-source voltage measurement, the MOSFET impedance \mi{Z}{Q} changes as the device switches between its off-state and on-state. 
Consequently, the reflection coefficient at the MOSFET end of the cable also varies during switching. 
If either termination is mismatched, the reflected wave travels back along the cable and may be reflected again at the opposite end. 
These repeated reflections can produce overshoot, ringing, and delayed settling, distorting the amplitude and shape of fast transitions; this effect becomes significant once the propagation delay is comparable with the transition time.

\begin{figure}[h]
	\centering\import{./figures/2_Fundamentals/}{SourceImpedanceMOSFET_cable.pdf_tex}
	\caption{Equivalent circuit illustrating cable reflections and the time-varying MOSFET impedance in a drain-source voltage measurement.}
	\label{fig:TransmissionLineMeasurementCable}
\end{figure}

Impedance matching, typically achieved by terminating the cable in its characteristic impedance, minimizes reflections at the matched termination.
Directly terminating a drain-source measurement in a low impedance would generally load the power circuit excessively.
An unmatched connection between the device and the sensing element should therefore be kept electrically short; this restriction does not preclude a matched cable after a suitable probe buffer or attenuator.
A common screening criterion is
\begin{equation}
    l < \frac{\lambda_{\min}}{10} = \frac{\mi{c}{W}}{10 f_{\max}},
\end{equation}
where $f_{\max}$ is the highest frequency of interest and $\lambda_{\min}$ is the corresponding wavelength in the cable.

	\caption{Simulation results of voltage measurement with \SI{1}{m} and \SI{50}{\Omega} coaxial cable.}
	\label{fig:Fundamental_Cable_TDFD}

\fref{fig:Fundamental_Cable_TDFD} shows the simulated effect of a \SI{1}{m} coaxial cable with a characteristic impedance of \SI{50}{\Omega}.
Although the main transition remains similar, the subsequent oscillation is strongly distorted.
The spectrum exhibits additional resonances associated with cable reflections.
The round-trip delay defines the characteristic frequency spacing $f_{\mathrm{W}} = \sfrac{\mi{c}{W}}{2 l}$, which is \SI{133}{MHz} for the propagation velocity used in this simulation; the termination impedances determine the precise resonance positions and amplitudes.

\subsubsection*{Common-Mode Rejection}
The common-mode voltage \mi{v}{cm} is the voltage component shared by both differential-input terminals relative to the measurement ground. 
The differential voltage is their difference. Ideally, a differential measurement rejects the shared component, but finite common-mode rejection allows part of it to affect the measured signal.
For linear operation, the common-mode rejection ratio (CMRR) quantifies this rejection:
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{CMRR}(f) = \left|\frac{\mi{A}{diff}(f)}{\mi{A}{cm}(f)}\right|,
\end{equation}
where \mi{A}{diff} and \mi{A}{cm} are the differential-mode and common-mode transfer functions.
CMRR is a linear amplitude ratio here; its value in decibels is $20\log_{10}(\mathrm{CMRR})$.
For each frequency component, the magnitude of the input-referred error is
\begin{equation}
    |\mi{E}{cm}(f)| = \frac{|\mi{V}{cm}(f)|}{\mathrm{CMRR}(f)}.
\end{equation}

Common-mode rejection is particularly demanding for a high-side gate-source measurement: the source terminal follows the switching-node voltage, whose excursion can greatly exceed the gate-source voltage of interest.

An additional disturbance, particularly relevant to low-side voltage and shunt measurements, is ground bounce.
The commutation current \mi{i}{S} flowing through a shared return-path inductance \mi{L}{\sigma} produces an approximate reference-potential displacement
\begin{equation}
    \mi{v}{cm} \approx \mi{L}{\sigma}\ddt{\mi{i}{S}}.
\end{equation}
This ground bounce is not necessarily negligible, as the shunt signal itself can also be small. 

If common-mode conversion can be approximated by a frequency-independent gain over the relevant band, a time-domain screening criterion is
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{CMRR} \geq \frac{\max \left|\mi{v}{cm}(t)\right|}{e_{\max}}.
\end{equation}
The maximum permissible input-referred error $e_{\max}$ follows from the required measurement accuracy.
One possible allocation is one least significant bit (LSB) of the oscilloscope's ADC, referred through the probe gain to the input; this value depends on the selected vertical range and is not a universal accuracy limit.

\subsubsection*{Summary of Measurement Errors and Verification Criteria}

\tref{tab:dynamic_measurements} summarizes the error mechanisms and their verification criteria.
Probe characterization establishes impedance, linear response, and large-signal limits, but its conclusions must be checked under the loading, coupling, and common-mode conditions of the intended setup.
Comparative measurements are most informative when the reference has independently characterized errors and does not share the same dominant coupling paths.
