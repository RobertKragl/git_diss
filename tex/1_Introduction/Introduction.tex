\chapter{Electromagnetic Emissions as a Design Metric for Power Semiconductor Devices}
\label{ch:Introduction}


\subsection*{Motivation}

% Semiconductor device design and electromagnetic compatibility (EMC) are two domains of power electronics that are frequently treated as distinct, despite their fundamental interdependence.
% Although semiconductor device switching is the primary source of conducted and radiated emissions, EMC remains predominantly addressed as a system-level problem rather than as an intrinsic semiconductor design variable.
% This thesis is positioned directly at this interface, which becomes apparent already in the fundamental design trade-offs of power electronic systems.

The design of power electronic systems inherently involves trade-offs among several competing objectives: efficiency, compactness, reliability, cost, and EMC~\cite{ieee_industry_applications_society_ieee_2024,rosado_design_2006}. 
Historically, efficiency and power density are prioritized during the primary design phase, while EMC is treated as a late-stage verification issue.

However, this traditional design paradigm is becoming increasingly challenging with the adoption of wide-bandgap (WBG) semiconductors, such as silicon-carbide (SiC) MOSFETs and gallium-nitride (GaN) devices. 
WBG devices feature low conduction and switching losses, enabling faster switching transients, but these faster transients also emit stronger EM noise. 
Since the applicable EMC standards and limits remain unchanged, these higher emissions directly complicate EMC compliance.
Additionally, the efficiency gains of WBG technologies enable highly compact converter designs. This physical miniaturization reduces the spatial distances between active components, cooling structures, and mechanical housings. 
Consequently, parasitic capacitive and inductive coupling paths are strengthened, further exacerbating the propagation of electromagnetic noise throughout the system.

Because EMC is still addressed only after the electrical and physical layout has been finalized, corrective measures become increasingly expensive as emissions worsen. 
Redesigning a non-compliant converter includes changes to the gate-drive, commutation loop, passive components, cooling concept, mechanical construction, and input/output filtering. 
These late-stage modifications typically require iterative prototyping and additional qualification cycles, which dramatically increase development time and costs. 
Furthermore, they frequently compromise previously optimized electrical, thermal, and mechanical design targets.

Faced with tight market deadlines, engineers often resort to a common late-stage remedy: slowing down the semiconductor switching speed by increasing the gate resistance. 
While this effectively reduces high-frequency emissions, it directly increases switching losses and degrades efficiency. 
Thus, late-stage mitigation represents a severe design compromise rather than an optimal engineering solution.

Yet no established methodology characterizes semiconductor devices at this early stage: conventional EMC measurements evaluate only the finished system and cannot isolate the intrinsic behavior of an individual device. If the EM emissions of power semiconductor devices could be characterized early, at the component level, this would open up two key opportunities:

(1) Semiconductor Device Design: 
From a manufacturing perspective, semiconductor fabrication and packaging offer numerous degrees of freedom, including doping profiles, metallization, cell geometries, and package-related parasitic parameters. 
While these parameters are traditionally optimized to minimize conduction and switching losses, they also heavily influence EM emissions. 
Identifying device-level parameters that reduce EM emissions without penalizing efficiency would allow the EMC behavior to be optimized as an additional design objective, avoiding the loss penalty of the late-stage gate-resistance remedy described above.

(2) Device Selection and Second-Source Qualification: 
Choosing among competing semiconductor devices, whether during the initial design of a converter or when qualifying an alternative supplier, introduces significant uncertainty regarding the resulting EMC performance.
Devices with near-identical nominal switching losses can still vary considerably in their switching transients and thus in their EM emissions, yet this variation is rarely quantified at the point of selection.
Consequently, second-source qualification and device benchmarking rely on repeating the complete system-level EMC compliance test for each candidate device, increasing both cost and development time.

Both opportunities, however, share the same prerequisite: resolving the intrinsic emissions of individual switching events directly at the component level.
Conventional system-level EMC measurements cannot provide this resolution: designed for compliance testing, they inherently blend the emissions of all noise sources within the system, making it impossible to attribute a measured spectral component to a specific device, package, or operating parameter.

This thesis directly addresses this gap by transitioning EMC characterization from a late-stage compliance constraint into an early-stage, quantitative design metric.

To this end, the thesis introduces a component-level measurement methodology designed to isolate and characterize the intrinsic electromagnetic emissions directly at the semiconductor terminals, while maintaining a traceable link to system-level EMC behavior. 
The developed methodology is generally applicable to all semiconductor technologies, including silicon IGBTs, SiC MOSFETs, GaN devices, and future device architectures. 
However, the experimental validation in this work focuses on discrete SiC MOSFETs.

\subsection*{Research Objectives}

The preceding considerations lead to the central objective of this thesis: to establish a measurement methodology to characterize the intrinsic high-frequency EM emissions of power semiconductor devices. 

To achieve this overall objective, the thesis pursues the following research objectives:
\begin{enumerate}
	\item To establish the relationship between semiconductor switching behavior and electromagnetic emissions, with particular consideration of the frequency range in which idealized switching-waveform models are no longer sufficient.
	\item To develop a measurement methodology that characterizes the high-frequency signal components directly at the power terminals of the switching devices with sufficient bandwidth and signal-to-noise ratio.
	\item To measure the three relevant, intrinsic noise sources of a switching cell separately, namely the high-side voltage, low-side voltage, and commutation current, so that their individual contributions to the overall emission behavior can be analyzed.
	\item To develop and apply signal-processing methods that reduce the measurement noise floor, separate relevant switching events, and extend the usable analysis range.
	\item To integrate the sensors and processing methods into a measurement platform that permits a cross-check between intrinsic-emission measurements and conventional system-level EMC measurements, such as LISN measurements.
	\item To demonstrate how the resulting event-resolved measurements can support the comparison of semiconductor devices and the identification of device or process parameters that influence electromagnetic emissions.
\end{enumerate}

The final objective is not to replace conventional EMC compliance testing, but to complement it with a component-level characterization method that can be applied earlier in the development process. 
This enables semiconductor, component, and converter engineers to assess EMC-relevant behavior before the complete system design has been finalized.


\subsection*{Thesis Structure}

Chapter~\ref{ch:Fundamentals} introduces the fundamentals of EMC in power electronic systems and establishes the relationship between the switching behavior of power semiconductor devices and their electromagnetic emissions. 
It distinguishes between noise generation at the semiconductor terminals and noise propagation through the surrounding converter. 
The chapter also examines errors in dynamic voltage and current measurements and establishes criteria for verifying the measurement sensors.
This chapter provides the physical and analytical basis for semiconductor and device engineers who seek to include EMC in the development of new technologies and device structures.

Chapter~\ref{ch:Methods} develops the methodology for measuring the EMC-relevant signal components of transient switching events. 
It first reviews existing approaches to semiconductor-focused emission characterization and identifies the methodological gap addressed in this thesis.
It addresses the limitations of conventional measurements at high frequencies and introduces the concepts used to improve the usable signal-to-noise ratio, isolate the relevant frequency components, and separate individual switching events. 
The chapter also defines the figures of merit and evaluation procedures used in the subsequent experimental investigations. 

Chapter~\ref{ch:Sensors} presents the hardware developed to realize the measurement methodology: a dual-channel differential active voltage probe for the high-side and low-side switching-node voltages, a complementary dual-channel active current probe for the commutation current, and the measurement platform that integrates both sensors into a representative power-electronic application, including a conventional line impedance stabilization network (LISN) for a crosscheck with classical system-level EMC measurements. 
For each sensor, the chapter derives the electrical requirements, describes the sensor architecture, and characterizes its measurement performance.

Chapter~\ref{ch:Validation} validates the resulting measurement environment on real switching events, demonstrating the impact of the individual noise-reduction steps and cross-referencing the intrinsic-emission measurements against the LISN measurement to confirm their consistency with established EMC practice.
This establishes the connection between the signals generated at the semiconductor terminals and the emissions observed at system level, which is relevant to converter and EMC engineers during system development and validation.
The chapter then applies the complete measurement methodology to the characterization of intrinsic SiC MOSFET emissions. 
The investigated parameters are divided into external parameters controlled by the application engineer and semiconductor parameters controlled by the device engineer. 
The influence of these parameters is evaluated under defined constraints, including constant switching losses, to enable a meaningful comparison of their EMC impact. 
The chapter demonstrates how the proposed method can be used to compare device variants, identify influential design parameters, and assess EM emissions as an additional semiconductor design metric.

Chapter~\ref{ch:Conclusion} summarizes the findings of the thesis and discusses their implications for semiconductor-focused EMC analysis. It evaluates the extent to which the measurement gap has been closed, identifies the practical value of the developed sensors and processing methods, and outlines future work toward improved sensors, systematic technology studies, high-frequency model validation, and the extension of the approach to other semiconductor technologies.

In this way, the thesis follows the path from the physical understanding of semiconductor emissions, through the development of a measurement methodology and its sensors, to component- and system-level demonstration and parameter studies.
