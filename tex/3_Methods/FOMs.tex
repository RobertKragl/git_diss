\section{Figures of Merit for Electromagnetic Emission Spectra}
Dependent on the specific use-case, it may be necessary to quantify the noise emission of a switching transient using a single value, known as a Figure of Merit (FOM).
One practical application is a noise analysis of a system over time. 
Performing a classical spectral analysis for this purpose can be challenging, as it results in complex 3D plots that can be difficult to interpret. 
In order to simplify the visualization of noise emission over time, a FOM can be employed to evaluate a single switching event. 
Unfortunately, there is not a single FOM, which can condense an entire spectrum plot into a single value. 
Instead, a combination of multiple FOMs must be utilized.

These FOMs can be categorized into two groups based on the specific area of the spectrum they provide information about.
The first category describes the broadband behavior of the spectrum, while the second category focuses on selective frequencies.
For power electronics, this is particularly the resonance between the output capacitance of the semiconductor and the parasitic inductance of the commutation cell.

\subsubsection*{\mi{t}{10-90\%}}
Most used, mainly because of the simplicity, is the rise time \mi{t}{r,10-90\%} of the signal.
It describes the time duration, which the signal needs to rise from 10\% of its final value to 90\%.

Fig. \ref{fig:IdealVsReal} shows the spectra for the drain-source voltage \mi{v}{DS} in a simple SPICE double pulse test compared to an often used simplification of an asymmetric first-order hold element \cite{costa_graphical_2005}.
This model reduces the switching event to the information of the rise and fall times.
It can be seen that the model describes the noise emission of \mi{v}{DS} well up to the second cutoff-frequency, where the slope decreases from \SI{-20}{dB/dec} to \SI{-40}{dB/dec}.
The two frequencies are defined as 
\begin{equation}
	\mi{f}{cut1} = \dfrac{1}{\pi \mathrm{d}T_\mathrm{s}} =  \SI{290}{kHz}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
	\mi{f}{cut2} = \dfrac{1}{\pi \cdot 1.25\cdot\mi{t}{10\%-90\%}} = \SI{12.7}{MHz}\\
\end{equation}

Above \mi{f}{cut2} the simulated \mi{v}{DS} falls below the first-order hold element, because the transitions into the slope have a smoothed behavior.
At \mi{f}{res} the output capacitance resonates with leakage inductance of the commutation loop

\begin{equation}
\mi{f}{res} = \dfrac{1}{2\pi \cdot \sqrt{\mi{C}{oss}\mi{L}{\sigma}}} = \SI{58}{MHz}.\\
\end{equation}

\subsubsection*{$\sddt{v}_\mathrm{max}$}
Another often used value is $\sddt{v}_\mathrm{max}$, which describes the differential at the steepest point of the transient.
However, it has no direct representation in the spectrum.
The voltage differential $\mathrm{d}v$ correlates with the system voltage \mi{V}{DC}, which is the starting value in the spectrum

\begin{equation}
	\mi{V}{DS}(f\rightarrow \SI{0}{Hz}) = \SI{20}{dB} \log(\dfrac{\mathrm{d}\mi{V}{DC}}{\SI{1}{\micro V}}) = \SI{175}{dB\micro V}
\end{equation}

And the time differential $\mathrm{d}t$ correlates with \mi{t}{10-90\%}.


\subsubsection*{Heisenberg-Gabor Inequality (HGI)}
In \cite{patin_power_2015} a theoretical noise optimal transient waveform was described by a so called \textit{Heisenberg-Gabor Inequality}. 
It was shown, that the optimal spectrum can be achieved with a switching transient of
\begin{equation}
	g(t) = \frac{1}{\sigma_t \sqrt{2\pi}}\cdot e^{-\dfrac{t^2}{2\sigma_t^2}},
\end{equation}
where the resulting semiconductor voltage results from the convolution
\begin{equation}
	\mi{v}{DS,opt} = \mi{V}{DC}\cdot\mathrm{rect}(\frac{t}{\mathrm{d}T_\mathrm{s}}) * g(t)
\end{equation}
The resulting waveform for $\sigma_t = \SI{10}{ns}$ is shown in Fig. \ref{fig:IdealVsReal}.

In \cite{martinez-padron_definition_2022} this optimum was used to define a FOM for the emitted noise of switching events.
It is calculated from the transient function $\lambda = \frac{\text{d}\mi{v}{DS}}{\text{d}t}$, which is the derivative of the voltage transient.
This function is used to calculate both the time and frequency dispersion.

\begin{equation}
	\sigma_t = \sqrt{\frac{\int (t-\mi{t}{b})^2 \cdot |\lambda(t)|^2 \text{d}t}{\int |\lambda(t)|^2 \text{d}t}}\text{, with } \mi{t}{b} = \int t\cdot\lambda(t) \text{d}t
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
\sigma_f = \sqrt{\frac{\int (\omega-\mi{\omega}{b})^2 \cdot |\Lambda(t)|^2 \text{d}\omega}{\int |\Lambda(t)|^2 \text{d}\omega}}\text{, with } \mi{\omega}{b} = \int \omega\cdot\Lambda(t) \text{d}\omega
\end{equation}

Here, $\Lambda(\omega)$ is the Fourier Transform of $\lambda(t)$.
Both, can be used to calculate the FOM 
\begin{equation}
	HGI = \sigma_f \cdot \sigma_t \geq 0.5.
\end{equation}

HGI is derived from a theoretical approach, to minimize the noise spectrum components for higher frequencies.
It describes, how far away the transient waveform is from ideal switching.
Ideal switching is an S-formed Gauss-Integral.
HGI does not take into account that resonances occur after the switching event.

\subsubsection*{$\bar{V}_{\Delta f}$}
With the goal, to give a broadband FOM for frequencies above the second cutoff frequency, an average of all Fourier coefficients can be defined as:

\begin{equation}
	\bar{v}_{10-150 \mathrm{MHz}} = \frac{1}{\Delta f_{tot}} \sum_{n = n_{10 \mathrm{MHz}}}^{n_{150 \mathrm{MHz}}} \Delta f_n \cdot a_n
\end{equation}

The frequency band of interest $\Delta f$ can be chosen according to \mi{f}{sw} and \mi{f}{res}.

\subsubsection*{$\hat{V}_\mathrm{res}$}
Most problems in EMC occur due to peaks in the noise spectrum.
These can be either caused by the noise source \mi{v}{DS} itself, or by a pole in the transfer function TF(f) due to passive components or the layout.
$\hat{V}_\mathrm{res}$ is defined as the Fourier coefficient at a selective frequency.

The calculation and the respective information of all FOMs is summarized in Tab. \ref{tab:FOMs} and their influence on the spectrum is visualized in Fig. \ref{fig:FOM_in_spectrum}.

\subsubsection*{Single Pulse Analysis in Frequency Domain}
While the time domain FOMs can be calculated for individual transient events, the frequency domain FOMs are affected by the duty cycle. 
Therefore, analyzing a single transient does not provide meaningful results.
Instead, a moving filter is used to calculate the spectrum for n switching periods, thereby separating consecutive events, see Fig.~\ref{fig:Moving_FD_FOM}. 
Afterwards, the signal snippet is repeated multiple times to give a sufficient signal length for the FFT algorithm.
This analysis can be conducted on the original data or on generated signals of the "snap-and-mirror" function.
